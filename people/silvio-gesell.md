## Overview

**Silvio Gesell** (1862–1930) was a German-Argentine merchant, economist, and briefly the finance minister of the short-lived Bavarian Soviet Republic (1919), best known for founding **Freiwirtschaft** ("free economy"), a reform program combining **Freigeld** ("free money" — currency subject to a periodic holding charge, or [demurrage](/wiki/economic-rent/), meant to eliminate the liquidity premium that lets money-holders extract interest without risk) with **Freiland** ("free land" — nationalization or public leasing of land so that rent, like the interest Gesell attacked, would not accrue to private owners).[1] His major work, *Die natürliche Wirtschaftsordnung* (*The Natural Economic Order*, 1916), influenced the "stamp scrip" currency movements of the 1930s Depression (including the Wörgl, Austria experiment) and drew qualified praise from **John Maynard Keynes**, who in the *General Theory* (1936) called him "the strange, unduly neglected prophet Silvio Gesell" and credited his free-money theory with a genuine, if incomplete, insight into the nature of interest.[2]

## Freiland as Gesell Actually Designed It

Gesell's land plank, read in his own text, is a more specific — and in one respect more
un-Georgist — proposal than "public ownership" conveys. Under Free-Land the State
**purchases** all private land, compensating owners in full: the purchase price is each
parcel's rent capitalised at the mortgage rate of interest, paid to landowners in
interest-bearing State securities, "not one penny more or less." The rent, now flowing to
the treasury, exactly services the interest on that debt, since the debt is the rent
capitalised.[1] The land is then leased by public auction to the highest bidder, for terms
of one, five, or ten years or for life, with every inhabitant of the globe entitled to
compete.[1]

Two features distinguish this from George's program:

- **It is fully compensated.** George's mature position was that landowners had no just
  claim to compensation; Gesell's design buys them out at full capitalised value. That
  places Gesell at the opposite end of the compensation question from George, and closer to
  the compensated, transition-softening designs the wiki treats under [the transition
  wealth-shock objection](/wiki/lvt-transition-wealth-shock/). It also means that during the
  bond's life the rent funds debt service rather than public spending — the fiscal dividend
  George promised arrives only as the debt is retired.
- **The residual rent is a per-child dividend, not general revenue.** Gesell's own text
  directs that the rent received "goes to the public treasury and is distributed monthly in
  equal shares to mothers according to the number of their young children," explicitly
  without regard to a mother's origin.[1] This is a [citizen's dividend](/wiki/citizens-dividend/)
  in the strict sense — rent distributed directly rather than spent by the state — and an
  unusually early one, targeted at caregivers rather than paid per capita.

Free-Land in Gesell's usage is also territorial as well as tenurial: he treats the earth as
a single globe over which no nation holds prerogative, and derives from that a rejection of
tariffs and import duties.[1] That is the textual basis for the anti-protectionist plank he
shares with George.

## The Money-and-Goods Asymmetry

Gesell's monetary case rests on an asymmetry he states plainly: "The possessor of goods,
during the period of storage, invariably incurs a loss in quantity and quality," plus the
costs of storage, insurance and caretaking — which he puts at around 5% a year — while the
holder of money bears no equivalent depreciation at all.[1] Money's exemption from carrying
cost is, on his account, what lets its holder wait out the producer and charge interest for
parting with it. Freigeld's periodic holding charge is designed to impose on money the same
carrying cost that goods and land already bear, removing the advantage rather than
prohibiting the practice.

## Relation to Georgism

Gesell and [Henry George](/wiki/henry-george/) were contemporaries who reached overlapping conclusions about land — both held that land rent should not accrue to private owners and both opposed protectionism — but built their reform programs around different primary targets: George treated land value taxation as the central lever, funding public goods and curbing speculation through a single tax on land; Gesell treated *money* as the primary site of unearned income, arguing that the ability to hoard money without cost (unlike perishable goods or land, which incur costs of storage/improvement) gives money-holders structural power over producers, and proposed demurrage currency as the fix, with public land leasing as a complementary, secondary plank rather than the central instrument.[3] The sharper divergence, visible only in Gesell's own text rather than in summaries of it, is on compensation: George would expropriate land rent without buying out its holders, while Gesell's Free-Land begins by paying them the full capitalised value — see above. A 2026 comparative paper argues the two traditions are complements rather than rivals for a modern reform program addressing land, money, and taxation together — see [Loehr (2026)](/wiki/loehr-george-gesell-odd-couple/).[3]
