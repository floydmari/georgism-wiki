## Overview

**Silvio Gesell** (1862–1930) was a German-Argentine merchant, economist, and briefly the finance minister of the short-lived Bavarian Soviet Republic (1919), best known for founding **Freiwirtschaft** ("free economy"), a reform program combining **Freigeld** ("free money" — currency subject to a periodic holding charge, or [demurrage](/wiki/economic-rent/), meant to eliminate the liquidity premium that lets money-holders extract interest without risk) with **Freiland** ("free land" — nationalization or public leasing of land so that rent, like the interest Gesell attacked, would not accrue to private owners).[1] His major work, *Die natürliche Wirtschaftsordnung* (*The Natural Economic Order*, 1916), influenced the "stamp scrip" currency movements of the 1930s Depression (including the Wörgl, Austria experiment) and drew qualified praise from **John Maynard Keynes**, who in the *General Theory* (1936) called him "the strange, unduly neglected prophet Silvio Gesell" and credited his free-money theory with a genuine, if incomplete, insight into the nature of interest.[2]

## Relation to Georgism

Gesell and [Henry George](/wiki/henry-george/) were contemporaries who reached overlapping conclusions about land — both held that land rent should not accrue to private owners and both opposed protectionism — but built their reform programs around different primary targets: George treated land value taxation as the central lever, funding public goods and curbing speculation through a single tax on land; Gesell treated *money* as the primary site of unearned income, arguing that the ability to hoard money without cost (unlike perishable goods or land, which incur costs of storage/improvement) gives money-holders structural power over producers, and proposed demurrage currency as the fix, with public land leasing as a complementary, secondary plank rather than the central instrument.[3] A 2026 comparative paper argues the two traditions are complements rather than rivals for a modern reform program addressing land, money, and taxation together — see [Loehr (2026)](/wiki/loehr-george-gesell-odd-couple/).[3]
