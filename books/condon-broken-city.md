## Overview

*Broken City: Land Speculation, Inequality, and Urban Crisis* (2024) is a book by urban
planning scholar **Patrick M. Condon** (University of British Columbia) arguing that
housing crises in major metropolitan areas are driven primarily by **land value
speculation** rather than by housing-supply shortages or restrictive zoning. Condon draws
explicitly on [Henry George](/wiki/henry-george/)'s work, contending that "the public,
rather than private owners, should capture the increase in land prices stimulated by
economic growth" — since land-value increases stem from public infrastructure investment
and surrounding development, not from the effort of the landowner.

## The Argument Against the Supply-Side Consensus

The book directly challenges the now-dominant "just build more housing" account of urban
housing crises associated with economist Edward Glaeser and the broader YIMBY policy
consensus. Condon's counter-argument is that restrictive zoning is not the primary driver
of unaffordability, and that density deregulation — upzoning that allows more building on a
given parcel — mostly **inflates land values** rather than lowering housing costs, because
the anticipated development rights are quickly capitalized into the price of the underlying
land, letting developers and landowners capture the gain rather than passing savings to
buyers or renters. This is a direct application of the wiki's own [residual land
valuation](/wiki/residual-land-valuation/) logic: upzoning without land-value capture
raises what land is worth precisely because it raises what can be built on it, and that
increased worth is bid into land prices at the point of sale.

## The Proposed Remedy: Vienna's Model

Condon's constructive proposal centers on Vienna's approach: using rent control to suppress
land's speculative profitability, which in turn lets government purchase land at lower
(non-speculative) prices for allocation to nonprofit housing associations — a
land-value-capture strategy achieved indirectly, through price suppression and public
acquisition, rather than through a direct land value tax.

## Critical Reception

Urban-planning scholar **Susan S. Fainstein** (Harvard), reviewing the book in the
*International Journal of Urban and Regional Research* (31 October 2025), calls it "an
excellent primer on the drivers of housing prices," "well written," and "highly suitable
for class assignment," agreeing that Condon's central thesis is "generally sound and
deserves reiterating." Her criticisms are twofold: she faults the book for overlooking the
spatial-inequality scholarship of Henri Lefebvre, David Harvey, and Neil Smith, arguing
Condon thereby makes "greater claims to originality than warranted," and she judges that the
book "minimizes the supply problem and failures to lower the costs of housing
construction" — a criticism that cuts the opposite direction from Condon's own critique of
Glaeser, essentially arguing Condon has over-corrected against the supply-side account
rather than fully reconciling with it.

A separate, earlier review by **Francis K. Peddle** for the [Henry George Foundation of
Canada](/wiki/henry-george-foundation-canada/) (November 2024) also covers the book,
predating Fainstein's academic review by nearly a year — see that organization's page for
the citation.

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This is a contemporary, explicitly Georgist trade/academic book making the land-speculation
diagnosis of the housing crisis to a general urban-planning and policy audience — a useful
complement to the wiki's more technical [land value capture](/wiki/land-value-capture/) and
[speculative vacancy](/wiki/speculative-vacancy/) material, aimed at readers encountering
the argument through contemporary housing-crisis debates rather than through classical
economics. Fainstein's own review, from a scholar not otherwise aligned with Georgist
theory, functions as independent academic validation that the land-speculation diagnosis is
"generally sound" even from a critical outside perspective — while her supply-side criticism
is a useful honest counterweight the wiki should not omit.

## Nuances and Limits

- **This page is built from a review, not a full read of the book.** Claims about the
  book's specific arguments and the Vienna proposal are as characterized in Fainstein's
  review; a full independent read of the book itself was not conducted this session.
- **Fainstein's supply-side criticism is a live tension**, not resolved here: whether land
  speculation or supply restriction is the dominant driver of urban housing unaffordability
  remains genuinely contested in the literature the wiki surveys elsewhere (see [housing
  unaffordability is a land problem](/wiki/housing-unaffordability-is-a-land-problem/)).
- **The Vienna remedy is rent-control-based**, not a direct land value tax — worth noting
  since a reader might assume any Georgist-adjacent housing proposal defaults to LVT.
