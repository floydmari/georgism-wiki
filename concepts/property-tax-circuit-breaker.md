## Overview

A **property tax circuit breaker** gives a household a credit or refund when its property tax exceeds a set share of its income, so that the tax is "cut off" at an overload point. The name was coined in the 1960s by John Shannon of the US Advisory Commission on Intergovernmental Relations, by analogy with the electrical device that stops a circuit being overloaded.[1] Lincoln Institute researchers define circuit breakers as programmes that give households direct property tax relief that increases as household income declines, for a given tax bill.[1]

The relief is aimed at the household's burden, not at the property's value: the tax stays at the full rate and the state or local government keeps its revenue base, while the household recovers the part that is out of proportion to what it earns.

## How It Works

Lincoln distinguishes three formula types: **threshold**, **sliding-scale** and "quasi" circuit breakers.[2] A **threshold** programme pays the part of the bill above a stated percentage of income; Michigan's homestead credit, for example, uses a 3.2 per cent threshold, with copayments above it.[3] A **multiple-threshold** formula raises the percentage by income bracket, which Lincoln recommends because it directs more relief to the poorest households.[1][2] A **sliding-scale** programme gives a set percentage reduction by income bracket, whatever the bill, and so does not measure the tax burden itself.[2]

**Renters** are covered by treating a share of rent as property tax. Lincoln reports that the most common "rent equivalent" is 20 per cent (the range runs from 6 to 35 per cent), so a renter paying $10,000 a year is assumed to pay $2,000 in property tax.[1][2]

Many programmes add a **copayment** (the household keeps paying part of the excess, so local spending decisions still register), an **income ceiling** and a **benefit cap**.[1]

**How many states.** Lincoln counted 33 states plus the District of Columbia with one or more circuit breakers in 2008.[1] Its 2018 summary table lists 30 states plus DC.[3] The tax-policy group ITEP counts 29 states plus DC as of 2025, on its own classification of programmes; counts differ with how borderline programmes are classed.[4]

**Minnesota** is the standard example. Its Department of Revenue describes a homestead credit refund for homeowners, with a regular refund based on income and property tax (for 2025 income below $142,490) and a special refund for a net property tax increase of more than 12 per cent (and at least $100), and a renter's credit claimed on the income tax return.[5][6]

**Versus other relief.** A fixed-dollar homestead exemption gives every homeowner the same reduction regardless of income, and assessment caps mostly benefit owners whose homes have risen fastest in value.[1][7] Lincoln quotes the comprehensive study of Haveman and Sexton (2008), which found assessment limits "among the least effective, least equitable, and least efficient strategies available for providing property tax relief."[7] Lincoln also argues that age is a poor stand-in for burden: elderly and non-elderly homeowners both spend about 35 per cent of income on housing ownership costs, and it recommends covering all ages.[7]

## Why It Matters for the Georgist Case

The usual objection to a land value tax is that it would squeeze households with valuable land but little cash income ([LVT hurts the asset-rich, cash-poor](/wiki/lvt-hurts-asset-rich-cash-poor/)). Circuit breakers, alongside tax deferral, are a standard response. Because the credit is measured against income rather than against land value, the land tax keeps its base and its rate (the features [land value tax](/wiki/land-value-tax/) relies on to avoid distorting land use or being [capitalised](/wiki/tax-capitalization/) away) while the household facing a real cash-flow problem is compensated. Exemptions and caps do the opposite: they remove land value from the base, and caps such as California's [Proposition 13](/wiki/proposition-13/) tie the tax to the date of purchase rather than to the owner's means. The size of the cash-flow burden in the Georgist case is modelled in [distributional impacts of LVT](/wiki/cwc-distributional-impacts-lvt/).

ITEP, an advocacy think-tank, calls circuit breakers states' "best tools" for making property taxes affordable; that is its policy view, and its report does not discuss land value taxation.[4]

## Limits and Caveats

- **Take-up.** Relief must be claimed, typically each year, and Lincoln warns that participation will likely be low without an outreach effort and a simple application.[1]
- **Low ceilings and caps.** Lincoln notes that many programmes give little help because of low income ceilings or benefit limits: in 2008 almost three-quarters had ceilings below the national median household income of $50,223, and Oklahoma limited eligibility to incomes below $12,000 with relief capped at $200.[7] Limits not indexed to inflation erode over time.[1]
- **Funding.** Benefits depend on state appropriations, and pro-rating or cuts in some years have been recorded (Iowa, New Jersey, California).[7]
- **Not a full answer for LVT.** The evidence concerns existing property taxes; applying it to a land value tax is an extension by analogy, and the take-up and ceiling weaknesses apply there too.
