## Overview

Maryland has no land value tax and its largest city cannot adopt one without the General
Assembly's permission. Property-tax bills in the state already show land and improvements
separately, but Baltimore City and the 23 counties may set only a single real-property rate.
A 2026 bill to change that, with a Senate cross-file, received hearings and went no further; a
Baltimore City Council resolution introduced that summer asks for an informational hearing on
a split-rate shift; and the Department of Legislative Services' note on the bill records an
earlier attempt in 1994. The state's other encounter with rent taxation is its
[digital advertising tax](/wiki/maryland-digital-ad-tax/), the first in the United States,
which the wiki treats under the taxation of attention rents rather than land.

## The 2026 Enabling Bill

House Bill 78 of the 2026 session, sponsored by Delegate Vaughn Stewart, is titled "Property
Taxes – Authority of Counties to Establish Subclasses and Set Separate Rates for Land and
Improvements to Land." It would authorise the Mayor and City Council of Baltimore City or the
governing body of a county to establish subclasses of real property consisting of land and of
improvements and to set a separate rate for each; it contains no requirement that the shift be
revenue-neutral.[1] The bill was referred to the Ways and Means Committee and heard on 27
January 2026; the Senate cross-file, Senate Bill 457 sponsored by Senator Jim Rosapepe, was
heard by Budget and Taxation on 18 February. Neither advanced before the session ended.[2][3]

The fiscal and policy note explains why the state's own costs loomed over the debate. Because
the State Department of Assessments and Taxation values all real property in Maryland, giving
counties separate land and improvement rates would require changes to its systems, data storage
and staffing; under one set of assumptions state expenditures rise by about $39.1 million in
fiscal 2027 and $8.1 million in fiscal 2031, with counties reimbursing part of the cost.[4] The
same note sets out the comparators the sponsors drew on: twelve Pennsylvania cities and towns
using split rates, the largest Scranton and Harrisburg; four Virginia cities authorised but none
using the power; and Connecticut's pilot authority for up to three municipalities.[4] The Department of Legislative Services' fiscal note records that enabling legislation was passed in 1994 and vetoed by Governor William Donald Schaefer.[4]

## Baltimore

Baltimore City Council Resolution 26-0062R, introduced by Councilmember Zac Blanchard on 13
July 2026 and assigned to the Budget and Appropriations Committee, invites stakeholders to an
informational hearing to evaluate the benefits of a split-rate property tax, reciting that such
a tax would impose a higher rate on land value and that nearby Pennsylvania municipalities with
split rates see more building.[5] Baltimore's own real-property rate stands at $2.248 per $100 of assessed value, set by the annual property-tax ordinances for fiscal 2026 and 2027, with no distinction between land and improvements.[6] The advocacy group Baltimore Thrive, whose
organiser appeared with Blanchard on his council podcast in September 2026, describes the goal
as a land value tax shift that would cut bills for most homeowners and productive commercial
property while raising them on vacant and underused land; the group's account of Harrisburg's
results and of the enabling bill's prospects is its own, and the podcast gives no modelled
figures for Baltimore.[7]

## Significance

Maryland is one of the states where 2026's wave of enabling legislation reached hearings but
not passage, alongside Colorado, Maine, Minnesota, Ohio and New Hampshire, while New York,
Virginia and Kentucky expanded the legal possibility of land value taxation
([2026 state enablement wave](/wiki/2026-state-lvt-enablement-wave/)). Its case shows the
practical obstacle a centralised state assessment system puts in the way of a local two-rate
tax: the decision to split rates is local, but the valuation machinery that would have to
carry it is the state's. [Pennsylvania](/wiki/pennsylvania/), whose cities the Maryland note
cites, is the precedent both sides argue from.
