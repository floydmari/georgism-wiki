## Overview

Wales occupies an unusual position in contemporary Georgist debate: not an
implementation, and not a live legislative proposal, but the site of the most sustained
**official study** of land value taxation feasibility anywhere in the UK, running inside
the Welsh Government's local-government-finance-reform programme since **2017**.[1]
That programme has produced a stalled 2018 attempt at a narrower vacant-land tax, a
2019 technical feasibility assessment, a 2025 ministerial commitment to move "beyond
the theoretical," and a February 2026 pair of academic working papers reviewing the
international evidence and scoping how land would even be valued — but, as of this
page's last review, no completed field test of any valuation method and no LVT bill.

## Devolved Tax Powers and the Council Tax/NDR Baseline

Wales gained the two local property taxes it now administers — **Council Tax** on
residential property and **Non-Domestic Rates (NDR)** on commercial property — at
devolution in 1999, with primary legislative competence over both only since 2011.[1]
Council Tax bands still rest on **2003** property valuations (already one revaluation
newer than England's 1991-based bands — see the [United Kingdom](/wiki/united-kingdom/)
page and [Andy Burnham](/wiki/andy-burnham/)), and fund only around 15% of local
authority spending, with the balance made up mostly through a Welsh Government
Revenue Support Grant (£4.9bn for 2025–26).[1] Since the **Wales Act 2014**, Wales has
also administered its own **Land Transaction Tax** (replacing Stamp Duty Land Tax) and
a **Landfill Disposals Tax**, both run by the Welsh Revenue Authority (established 2017),
plus **Welsh Rates of Income Tax** from 2019 (administered by HMRC).[1] In 2018 Wales
selected a **vacant land tax** — narrower than a full LVT, but built on the same Georgist
logic of taxing idle land to encourage development — as a candidate for its first
entirely new devolved tax; it required UK Government sign-off, and discussions with HM
Treasury remained at an "impasse" as of 2024.[1]

## The 2025 Political Moment: Drakeford's Senedd Statement

On 11 June 2025, Wales's finance secretary **Mark Drakeford** told the Senedd that a
land value tax remained on the table as a long-term replacement for Council Tax and
business rates, pointing to Denmark and Singapore as international comparators.[2]
Drakeford framed the immediate task as closing the gap between theoretical appeal
and practical feasibility: "I am anxious to see this discussion move beyond the
theoretical and into the realms of the practically possible."[2] He confirmed the Welsh
Government had **invited tenders to test approaches to valuing land**, "with
submissions for every aspect of the work," adding: "I want to use the coming months to
test the boundaries of what might be possible in the next Senedd term... Let's open the
door to more radical, fundamental and progressive reform in the future."[2] Opposition
Senedd member Peter Fox agreed Council Tax "will never really become a fair local
tax" but pressed instead for reform of what he called the "outdated and flawed" local
government funding formula, to which Drakeford replied that all 22 Welsh local
authorities agree the formula needs revision but disagree on how.[2]

## The 2026 Evidence Review and Valuation-Methods Scoping

The first published research outputs of this renewed push arrived in February 2026: two
working papers by Dr Bryonny Goodwin-Hawkins (Countryside and Community
Research Institute), written under a UKRI Policy Fellowship on Welsh local government
finance reform. The first, a systematic review of 70 international empirical studies,
concluded that LVT's commonly cited efficiency and equity claims are "plausible in
theory but not strongly supported by direct empirical evidence," with real-world effects
"typically localised and context-dependent." The second identified five families of
land-valuation method (market-based statistical, algorithmic/machine-learning,
formula-based, conventional professional appraisal, and innovative/experimental)
as candidates for testing in Wales, concluding "no single optimal method exists." Full
findings, the seven claims tested, and an important honest-limits point about how this
programme's status has been characterised elsewhere on the wiki live on the dedicated
research page: [A Land Value Tax for Wales? The Welsh Government Valuation-Testing
Programme](/wiki/welsh-lvt-valuation-testing/).

## Wales's Deeper Georgist Connection: David Lloyd George

Wales's most consequential historical link to land value taxation predates this
programme by well over a century. **[David Lloyd George](/wiki/david-lloyd-george/)**,
architect of the UK's **[1909 People's Budget](/wiki/1909-peoples-budget/)** land value
duties and its Limehouse defence of them, was raised from early childhood in
Llanystumdwy, Caernarfonshire, and represented the Welsh constituency of Caernarfon
Boroughs in Parliament for 55 years. The full account of his career and the 1909
campaign lives on his own page and the [United Kingdom](/wiki/united-kingdom/) place
profile, per the wiki's one-finding-one-home rule; this page notes the connection rather
than duplicating it.
