## Overview

Texas has no land value tax, no split-rate property tax and no official study of either. Its Georgist record is a single episode, the [Houston Plan of Taxation](/wiki/houston-plan-of-taxation/), which was struck down in 1915 under the state constitution. Two features of current Texas law shape any reform argument today: a constitutional rule that real property be taxed in proportion to its value, and a statutory limit on how fast a city's operating property-tax revenue can grow without a vote of the people. Both are state rules, so this page treats the state as the unit and uses Austin as a worked case.

## The Constitutional Barrier

Article VIII, section 1 of the Texas Constitution opens with "Taxation shall be equal and uniform" (subsection (a)). Subsection (b) provides that real property and tangible personal property, unless exempt, "shall be taxed in proportion to its value, which shall be ascertained as may be provided by law."[1]

The Tax Code implements this on a single basis. Property is appraised at market value as of January 1 (§23.01), and "all property shall be assessed on the basis of 100 percent of its appraised value" (§26.02).[2][3] Nothing in those provisions lets a taxing unit set a different fraction or rate for land than for buildings.

The 1915 ruling in *Baker v. City of Houston*, which struck down the [Houston Plan](/wiki/houston-plan-of-taxation/) under the proportionality language, is told on that page; the language remains in the current constitution.

As a statement about the text, a land-only or split-rate tax levied by Texas local governments would assess land and improvements on different bases from each other, which §26.02 and Article VIII, section 1(b) do not on their face permit. No court or state body has tested whether a modern design, such as the building exemption discussed for [Washington State](/wiki/washington-state/), could be fitted within the clause.

## The 3.5 Percent Revenue Cap and Tax-Rate Elections

Under Tax Code §26.04, a city's voter-approval tax rate is its no-new-revenue maintenance-and-operations rate multiplied by 1.035, plus its current debt rate and any unused increment rate.[4] The practical effect is a 3.5 percent annual ceiling on operating revenue from property already on the rolls; new construction is outside it, and debt service is added on top.

Under §26.07, a municipality of 30,000 or more that adopts a rate above the voter-approval rate must submit it to voters at a November election. If the proposition fails, the rate for that year is the voter-approval rate.[5]

These provisions come from Senate Bill 2, the Texas Property Tax Reform and Transparency Act of 2019, which the governor signed on 12 June 2019 and which took effect in the relevant sections on 1 January 2020.[6][7] Press coverage describes the previous ceiling for cities as 8 percent.[8]

## A Worked Austin Example

**The budget.** The City of Austin's approved FY2026-27 budget sets the General Fund at $1,543,720,835. Property tax supplies $743.9 million of it, 48.2 percent of General Fund revenue, within total property-tax revenue of $1,282.5 million; the remainder goes to Project Connect transit, debt service and tax-increment zones. The approved overall rate is $0.579948 per $100 of value, against $0.524017 the year before, and equals the city's voter-approval rate.[9]

**Proposition Q.** In November 2025 Austin put to voters, under Ordinance 20250813-010, a rate of $0.574017 per $100, which was $0.05 above the voter-approval rate.[10] The proposition failed on 4 November 2025 with more than 63 percent voting against, according to election-night counts reported by Community Impact; those totals were unofficial pending canvass.[11] KUT's reporting described the margin as roughly two to one.[12] Austin's FY2026-27 rate was then set at the maximum allowed without voter approval, and a council policy bars seeking a larger increase until at least 2029.[13] Appraisal-district projections reported in April 2026 had Travis County taxable value in Austin falling 3.1 percent to $210.6 billion in 2026, after a 2.7 percent fall in 2025, with new construction adding $2.8 billion against a peak of nearly $5.5 billion two years earlier.[8]

**Two parcels.** The Travis Central Appraisal District's 2026 certified values allow a concrete comparison.[14]

| | Waterline tower, 98 Red River St | Parking lot, 604 Driskill St |
|---|---|---|
| Area | 3.071 acres | 1.397 acres |
| Land value | $107.0M | $45.6M |
| Improvement value | $513.6M | $27,875 |
| Total | $620.6M | $45.7M |
| Land value per acre | about $34.9M | about $32.7M |
| City tax per acre at the no-new-revenue rate ($0.535466) | about $1.08M | about $175k |

Land value per acre is nearly equal (a ratio of 1.07), while tax per acre differs by about 6.2 to 1, because the tower's improvements are taxed along with its land. The parking lot's improvement record is a paved area.

The comparison originates with Ryan Puzycki, who writes the *City of Yes* newsletter on Austin urbanism; the appraisal records carry the figures.[15] He also illustrates a pure land value tax on the pair at roughly $650,000 and $610,000 per acre. Those figures equal-weight the two parcels per acre. Weighted by actual acreage, a revenue-neutral land-only tax on the pair would come to about $814,000 and $764,000 per acre; the qualitative point, near-equal bills for near-equal land, holds either way.

TCAD already carries separate land and improvement values for each parcel, which bears on the [objection that land cannot be assessed](/wiki/land-cannot-be-assessed/); those land values are mass-appraisal estimates, not individual site valuations. The pair also illustrates [speculative vacancy](/wiki/speculative-vacancy/) in its under-use form, though two parcels are an illustration, not a sample.

## Evidence from Texas

[Plummer (2010)](/wiki/plummer-lvt-distribution/) simulated a revenue-neutral land value tax on Tarrant County (Fort Worth) parcel data for 1997 to 2006. Average single-family tax liability fell by roughly 30 percent, with the burden moving to commercial, utility and especially vacant land; the effect on progressivity within residential property was slight. It is a simulation on one county's assessment roll, not an implemented policy.

## Nuances and Limits

- No Texas jurisdiction has adopted or officially studied a land value tax or split-rate tax since 1915.
- The Austin comparison is one pair of parcels, not a citywide estimate; effects on homeowners would need parcel-level modeling of the whole city.
- The parking lot is identified by matching the published per-acre figures to the appraisal district's records, since the original post does not name the parcel. It is a lot in the same downtown district; its position relative to the tower has not been confirmed.
- The Prop Q margin is from election-night counts and not from the certified canvass.
- The Tax Code text cited is a third-party mirror current to May 2025, not the official code; later amendments have not been checked.
- The per-acre tax uses the no-new-revenue rate and counts only the city's levy; at the adopted rate the parcels would pay about $1.17M and $190k per acre, with the ratio unchanged.
