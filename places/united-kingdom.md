## Overview

The United Kingdom occupies an outsized place in Georgist history and evidence. It supplies the movement's founding grievance (the enclosure of common land), its most consequential legislative near-miss (the 1909 People's Budget), its most ambitious and most cautionary attempt at capturing land-value uplift through planning law (the 1947 Town and Country Planning Act), and — in the twenty-first century — some of the clearest official statistics anywhere on just how large a share of national wealth land actually represents. The UK's Office for National Statistics estimated land at **£6.3 trillion**, or "nearly three-fifths of the UK's net worth" of £10.7 trillion, in its most recent balance-sheet data as of a March 2022 report — with households alone owning £4.3 trillion of that land value.[1]

## Enclosure and the Roots of Land Concentration

Modern British land concentration traces substantially to **parliamentary enclosure**, the process by which common and open-field land was converted to private, individually-owned holdings. Simon Fairlie's history for *The Land* magazine estimates that roughly 7 million acres — about one-sixth of England — were enclosed through approximately 4,000 individual Acts of Parliament between roughly 1750 and 1850, with parliamentary committees reviewing enclosure bills dominated by MPs who were themselves local landowners.[2] The consequence, Fairlie argues, is durable: in contemporary Britain, "nearly half the country is owned by 40,000 land millionaires, or 0.06 per cent of the population."[2] See [A Short History of Enclosure in Britain](/wiki/fairlie-short-history-enclosure/) for the fuller account, including the Scottish Clearances and the historiographical debate over whether enclosure was economically necessary.

## The Georgist High-Water Mark: The 1909 People's Budget

Britain came closer than any other major economy to enacting a national land value tax during the **[1909 People's Budget](/wiki/1909-peoples-budget/)**, introduced by Chancellor **[David Lloyd George](/wiki/david-lloyd-george/)** with **[Winston Churchill](/wiki/winston-churchill/)** campaigning alongside him. The budget's land value duties passed the Commons but were rejected by the landowner-dominated House of Lords, triggering a constitutional crisis, two general elections in 1910, and the **[Parliament Act 1911](/wiki/parliament-act-1911/)**, which permanently removed the Lords' veto over money bills. The land duties themselves proved complex and under-yielding and were later repealed, but the episode remains, in the words of the wiki's own event page, "the most consequential land-tax campaign in British history."

## The 1947 Planning Settlement

Britain's other landmark attempt at capturing land value came through planning law rather than taxation. The **[Town and Country Planning Act 1947](/wiki/town-and-country-planning-act-1947/)** nationalized development rights — any change of land use required planning permission — and imposed a **100% development charge** on the value uplift that permission created, the most ambitious legislated betterment-capture scheme in British history. Set at a confiscatory rate, the charge removed any incentive to bring land forward for development and was repealed by the incoming Conservative government's 1953 and 1954 Planning Acts, leaving permanent planning control in place without the accompanying value-capture mechanism — a combination that later analysts link to Britain's chronically restricted land supply. See [Betterment Levy](/wiki/betterment-levy/) for the wider pattern this episode illustrates.

## The Contemporary Housing Crisis

Ryan-Collins, Lloyd and Macfarlane's *Rethinking the Economics of Land and Housing* (2017) documents the scale of the resulting affordability problem: UK house prices have risen roughly **five times** in real terms since the Second World War, while land prices underlying them have risen roughly **fifteen times** over the same period (Ch. 1, Fig. 1.1).[3] The house price-to-income ratio rose from about 5 in 1996 to over 10 by 2007 (Ch. 4, §4.4), and UK residential property values reached over 300% of GDP, up from a historical range of 60–180% (Ch. 1, Fig. 1.2).[3] The authors trace this partly to the 1947 settlement's incomplete legacy — planning constraint without betterment capture — combined with a land–credit feedback loop in which mortgage lending against land as collateral inflates land prices, which in turn expands banks' collateral base for further lending. Lars Doucet's *Land Is a Big Deal* draws on the same UK evidence as part of its broader case that land, not structures, drives urban real-estate value worldwide.[4]

## The 2026 LVT Moment: Andy Burnham's Premiership

Land value taxation returned to the centre of British politics in 2026. Campaigning to succeed Keir Starmer as Labour leader, then-Mayor of Greater Manchester **[Andy Burnham](/wiki/andy-burnham/)** told *The Times* (22 May 2026) that he had "long been persuaded of the argument for a Land Value Tax" and wanted to reform a council tax system he called "highly regressive" and still built on "not justifiable" 1991 property valuations.[5] Coverage through June 2026 attached Burnham to Fairer Share's proposal for a 0.48% annual proportional property tax (on land and buildings together, though widely covered under the "land tax" banner) replacing both council tax and stamp duty, with estimates that a UK-wide levy of this scale could raise on the order of £35 billion a year — alongside serious warnings, from figures such as former Bank of England economist Charles Goodhart, about the transition risk of moving quickly on such a large asset-price shift.[5] The debate also drew a public intervention from **Murad Qureshi**, Chair of the **[Labour Land Campaign](/wiki/labour-land-campaign/)**, whose 29 May 2026 *Financial Times* letter urged Burnham not to hesitate on LVT as a response to stagnant growth and a post-2008 shift of wealth toward asset owners.[5] Burnham was elected Labour leader unopposed on 17 July 2026 and became Prime Minister on 20 July 2026; as of this page's last review it remained undecided whether, or in what form, his government would legislate an LVT. Full timeline, figures, and sourcing live on the [Andy Burnham](/wiki/andy-burnham/) page, per the wiki's one-home-per-finding rule. Devolved [Wales](/wiki/wales/) has run its own, longer-running parallel inquiry into LVT as a council-tax and business-rates replacement, most recently a February 2026 government-adjacent evidence review and valuation-methodology scoping exercise — see [the dedicated research page](/wiki/welsh-lvt-valuation-testing/) for findings.

## "Is Land Undertaxed in Britain?" — The Leunig Exchange

Burnham's premiership sharpened a prior question that the British debate had mostly assumed an answer to. Shortly before he took office, Burnham restated his belief that land is undertaxed in Britain; **Tim Leunig**, chief economist at the research foundation Nesta and author of a rival property-tax reform proposal, pushed back — cautioning against treating land as "a big cash cow" and saying that while land is "atrociously badly taxed" in Britain, "it is not the case that land is undertaxed in Britain."[6]

The pushback has an obvious statistic behind it. The UK raises about **2.8% of GDP** from annual property taxes, second in the OECD only to Canada; add stamp duties and the figure rises to roughly **3.7%**, the highest in the OECD. Since land is well over half of UK property value, the inference that British land cannot really be undertaxed looks close to arithmetic.

An August 2026 [Henry George Foundation](/wiki/henry-george-foundation-uk/) essay argues the inference does not survive disaggregation, and its reasoning is the most useful part for this wiki because it generalises past Britain. Three steps:

- **Commercial and residential land are taxed at very different effective rates.** Business rates fall on produced inputs — buildings, plant, machinery — which the Mirrlees Review identified as inefficient because it distorts firms' production choices and penalises long-term fixed-capital investment. Simple abolition would hand commercial property owners a windfall capitalised into land prices, so the Review's own recommendation was replacement by a commercial [LVT](/wiki/land-value-tax/), at a rate "somewhere in the region of 4%" of capitalised land values to be revenue-neutral against the £27.6 billion business rates raised in 2025-26. That rate is far above the current *effective* rate on commercial land — so on the essay's reading business rates are destructive precisely because they leave too much commercial land value untaxed: "Produced inputs like buildings, plant, and machinery are *over* taxed because commercial land is *under* taxed."[6]
- **Revenue as a share of GDP is close to irrelevant to the question.** The essay's sharpest move is a comparison case: US residential property taxes are also well above the OECD average, yet the US tax system as a whole is heavily biased *toward* owner-occupied housing, through the $250,000/$500,000 capital-gains exclusion, deductibility of property tax against federal income tax, and the non-taxation of [imputed rent](/wiki/imputed-rent/). High gross property-tax revenue coexists with radically undertaxed returns to residential landownership, because the offsets sit in other parts of the code.
- **The UK's offsets are larger still.** Principal private residences are entirely exempt from capital gains tax, VAT does not apply to owner-occupied housing services, and imputed rental income is untaxed. Meanwhile council tax, banded on 1991 valuations in England and Scotland, can exceed 1% of property value in the North East and fall to as low as 0.02% in central London — regressive by location, and in the lowest-value areas effectively a tax on structures rather than on land, since land value there is near zero. The essay's conclusion is that a revenue-neutral swap of council tax and SDLT for an LVT "would barely cover" the VAT-exemption imbalance alone, so **residential land would remain significantly undertaxed relative to other return-yielding assets even after an LVT was introduced**.[6]

The essay is advocacy rather than neutral analysis, and its policy preferences are explicit — it proposes using some residential-LVT revenue to cut National Insurance contributions rather than to replace council tax wholesale, and endorses the Tax Policy Associates warning that an LVT introduced without regard to elderly homeowners, landlords, banks and pension funds could be catastrophic. Its argumentative core is nonetheless independent of that: **cross-country comparisons of property-tax revenue as a share of GDP cannot settle whether land is undertaxed, because they omit the treatment of land returns everywhere else in the tax code.** That point bears directly on the wiki's [LVT can't raise enough revenue](/wiki/lvt-not-enough-revenue/) and [LVT is just a property tax](/wiki/lvt-is-just-a-property-tax/) pages, and it is the reason Leunig and Burnham can both be describing the same tax system accurately while disagreeing about it.
