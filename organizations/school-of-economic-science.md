## Overview

The **School of Economic Science** — renamed the **School of Philosophy and Economic Science
(SPES)** in 2019 — is a London-headquartered educational organization with a genuinely
Georgist founding that later evolved into a broader philosophy and meditation school. It
matters to this wiki for two separate reasons that should not be conflated: its members remain
active, credentialed contributors to UK land-value-tax policy work today (see [Andrew
Purves](/wiki/andrew-purves/) and the [Coalition for Economic
Justice](/wiki/coalition-for-economic-justice/)), and — separately — the organization carries a
documented history of serious controversy, including child-abuse findings at affiliated
schools and decades of "cult" characterization in the British press and by former members. This
page reports both, following the wiki's practice of reporting organizational history honestly
rather than omitting the uncomfortable parts.

## Founding and Georgist Origins

Sources give slightly different founding years and initial names, which this page reports
rather than resolves. Per Wikipedia, the organization "was founded in the UK in 1938 by Andrew
MacLaren MP under the name Henry George School of Economics," initially "an economics study
group that explored the economic theories of the American economist Henry George," meeting in
a Parliamentary committee room of the Houses of Parliament.[1] SPES's own official history page
instead dates its founding to **1937**, states it was founded by **Leon MacLaren** (Andrew
MacLaren's son) rather than Andrew himself, and describes it as drawing on "the land-value
taxation ideas of Henry George" and "the earlier work of his father Andrew MacLaren, Member of
Parliament."[2] Both sources agree the organization was renamed the **School of Economic
Science** in 1942 and, in 2019, renamed again to the **School of Philosophy and Economic
Science**.[1][2]

## From Georgist Study Group to Philosophy School

Leon MacLaren inherited leadership from his father and progressively redirected the
organization's focus to "the study of natural laws governing the relations between men in
society."[1] The evolution proceeded through several distinct phases: in the early 1950s,
MacLaren introduced a Plato-based philosophy curriculum after concluding economics alone was
insufficient; in 1953 he encountered P.D. Ouspensky and G.I. Gurdjieff's "Fourth Way" teaching
via Dr. Francis Roles and the Study Society; from 1959 he met Maharishi Mahesh Yogi and began
practicing Transcendental Meditation, organizing a 1961 "World Congress" for the Maharishi at
the Royal Albert Hall attended by roughly 5,000 people, nearly all SES members; and through
Roles's contact with the Shankaracharya of Jyotir Math, Swami Shantanand Saraswati, the
organization's teaching became predominantly based on **Advaita Vedanta**, which remains its
central subject today under the name "Practical Philosophy."[1] SES has since phased out most
of its Gurdjieffian material, though it still acknowledges Gurdjieff and Ouspensky as sources
of its first philosophy courses.[1] Leon MacLaren died in the mid-1990s and was succeeded by
Donald Lambie; the organization "gradually underwent a change in approach, choosing to be more
open" after his death.[1]

## Scale and Finances

As of 2012, SES reported around 20,000 enrolments across roughly 80 branches in 18 countries,
including 3,173 enrolments in the UK.[1] Its UK organization reported 2017 income of £5.1m,
spending of £4.0m, £15.5m of owned-use assets, and £10.0m of long-term investments, with 9
trustees, 98 employees, and 500 volunteers.[1] Property holdings include the Waterperry Estate
in Oxfordshire (purchased 1972) and Nanpantan Hall in Loughborough (bequeathed 1986); a London
mansion, Sarum Chase in Hampstead, was sold in 2005 for £9.3 million.[1] SES's London
headquarters is at 11 Mandeville Place, freehold acquired in 2002[1][2] — the same address used
by the [Henry George Foundation (UK)](/wiki/henry-george-foundation-uk/), which operates "c/o
the School of Philosophy and Economic Science,"[3] and by the Coalition for Economic Justice's
OPDC Working Group as its contact address in a 2018-19 submission.[4]

## Continuing Land-Value-Tax Engagement

Despite its broader philosophical turn, SES has continued to sponsor Georgist policy work
directly. In 2017-18 it made a formal submission — co-authored by [Andrew
Purves](/wiki/andrew-purves/) with Stephen Stretton and Tony Vickers — to the Scottish Land
Commission's tender on international land-value-tax experience, published as a cover-story
article in the Henry George Foundation's journal *Land & Liberty*.[5] In March 2018, SES
members Dr Peter Bowman and Andrew Purves submitted formal written evidence, explicitly "on
behalf of the School of Economic Science," to a UK Parliament land value capture inquiry,
arguing existing UK land-value-capture mechanisms are "partial, contested, complex,
discretionary, costly to negotiate" and had captured only about £3.12 billion of an estimated
£12.4 billion in land value uplift in England in 2014-15 (roughly £9.3 billion uncaptured),
citing Hong Kong's MTR Corporation model as a comparison.[6] SES also hosts the [Coalition for
Economic Justice](/wiki/coalition-for-economic-justice/)'s steering-group meetings.[4]

## Controversy: Child-Abuse Findings

SES operated or supported several affiliated schools, including St James Independent Schools
(founded 1975) and St Vedast's School for Boys (operated mid-1970s to 1985). In the early
1980s, both schools received complaints from parents and were the subject of a critical series
of articles in the London *Evening Standard* focused on their harsh discipline regime and links
to SES, which the journalists described as a "cult."[1] In 2005, following complaints raised on
an internet message board for former pupils, the schools' governors initiated a private inquiry
chaired by British QC James Townsend. The inquiry found that from 1975 to 1985, children had
been "criminally assaulted" while attending the schools, including unreasonable punishment,
assault from teachers, and general mistreatment.[1] The school stated the named teachers "will
receive disciplinary warnings" but had "no plan to remove them"; per Wikipedia, the teachers
subsequently no longer worked at the schools.[1] The inquiry's findings were reported by a
Channel 4 News special on 15 March 2006 and in national and local press.[1] In December 2020,
the BBC reported that, following legal action launched in 2016, 45 former students who attended
St James and St Vedast between 1975 and 1992 had received settlements of up to £30,000 each,
settled without an admission of liability and never brought before a court; none of the former
pupils received an apology.[1]

A related institution, the Plato school in Amsterdam — founded by SES's Dutch branch with close
links to the UK St James schools — was investigated by Dutch police in 1996 and 2000 over
illegal physical punishment and mistreatment of children; its headteacher was prosecuted and
found guilty of assault, and the school closed in 2002 after the second investigation.[1]

## Controversy: "Cult" Characterization

SES has been repeatedly described as a cult, sect, or new religious movement, including by
several former members, though the organization and many members reject this characterization
and describe SES as a philosophy school rather than a religion.[1] The 1983 *Evening Standard*
series by journalists Peter Hounam and Andrew Hogg alleged SES was "a cult infiltrating the
Liberal Party" that "enforced a severe diet, persecuted women and kept its members closed off
from the outside world"; their 1984 book *Secret Cult* claimed the organization "aimed to
establish psychological control over its members and had caused personality change, mental
breakdown and divorce."[1] Journalist William Shaw's 1994 book *Spying in Guru Land: Inside
Britain's Cults* described attending SES and characterized its regime as "part Gurdjieffian
discipline, part oriental mysticism, part Christian mysticism, part social snobbery," while
also noting he did not personally witness brainwashing during the introductory course he
attended.[1] In 2009, actress and former St James pupil Clara Salaman published the novel
*Shame On You*, based substantially (by her own account, 75-85%) on her own childhood
experience of the school and SES, including accounts of abuse. In 2019, actress Emily Watson,
raised in SES, described it as "a quasi-religious organisation/cult" that taught members "to
think a certain way about the world, and about yourself, that is very denigrating to the
individual and to women," with "a sort of emotional cruelty that was utterly out of place in a
place of education that purports to be based on love and understanding."[1]

Named defenders and more measured accounts also appear in the record. Roger Pincham, a member
since 1955, wrote to the *Evening Standard* in 1982 challenging the Hounam/Hogg reporting as
based on "a few students" or a small number of attended sessions rather than a balanced view.[1]
SES member and author Brian Hodgkinson responded to the *Secret Cult* allegations by describing
the teaching's aim as freeing "the mind from the limitations imposed by the ego" and stressing
that "no actual force was used" and that "anyone can walk away from a School meeting or event at
any time."[1] Colin Slee, Provost of Southwark Cathedral, who had contributed material treating
SES as a cult in 1983, said by 1999 he had shifted his view and saw it instead as a "New
Religious Movement."[1] Writer Jules Evans, after his own 2013 investigation, concluded "I
personally don't think SES is a 'secret cult'. It has lost its charismatic and authoritarian
Leader. Its membership is declining."[1]

## Nuances and Limits

- **Founding-year and founder discrepancy unresolved.** SPES's own history page dates its
  founding to 1937 under Leon MacLaren; Wikipedia dates it to 1938 under Andrew MacLaren (Leon's
  father). This page reports both without resolving the discrepancy; a future editor with access
  to a primary institutional record (e.g., charity registration documents) could settle it.
- **The controversy content above is drawn from Wikipedia's own citations** (the 1983 *Evening
  Standard* series, the 1984 *Secret Cult* book, the 2005 Townsend inquiry, the December 2020
  BBC report, and named ex-member accounts), independently fetched and read directly this
  session rather than taken from a secondary paraphrase. Sourcewatch's parallel entry on SES was
  attempted but blocked by a bot-challenge to this session and is not cited directly here;
  Sourcewatch's content, per external search results, appears consistent with Wikipedia's
  account and additionally references a 1997 Belgian Parliamentary Commission report on cults,
  which this page does not independently verify.
- **This page does not attempt to adjudicate** whether SES today, in 2026, resembles the
  organization described in the 1980s-2000s controversy record. The most recent named
  assessment cited here (Jules Evans, 2013) found it declining and no longer led by a
  "charismatic and authoritarian Leader," but no more recent independent assessment was located
  this session.
- **SES's continuing Georgist policy engagement (Purves, Bowman, the Scottish Land Commission
  submission, the parliamentary evidence) is documented independently of, and is not evidence
  against or in favor of, the controversy record above** — the two threads of this page's
  history are kept separate deliberately.
