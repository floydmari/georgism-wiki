## Summary

"Henry George and Clark's Paradigm: The Erasure of Land in Economic Theory (A
Historical-Philosophical Analysis)," by **Niels Charlier** and **Nicolaus Tideman**
(Virginia Tech), is a HAL preprint (September 2025). It makes the historical case, in far
more depth than the wiki's existing pages summarize, for a claim the wiki's [objection: land
is just capital](/wiki/land-is-just-capital/) page already traces to John Bates Clark: that
the merger of land into "capital" in mainstream economic theory was not a neutral scientific
advance but a targeted response to [Henry George](/wiki/henry-george/)'s *Progress and
Poverty* (1879).

## The Argument

The paper's central claim is that Clark's *Capital and Its Earnings* (1888) — which
eliminated land as a distinct factor of production, leaving only labor and capital — was
written in direct reaction to George. The evidence assembled includes:

- **Contemporary testimony from an unfriendly witness.** Frank Fetter (1927) — himself an
  opponent of George and a Clark ally, which the authors note makes his testimony more
  credible, not less — wrote that the "probable source from which immediate stimulation came
  to Clark was the contemporary single tax discussion," adding that one can "hardly fail to
  see on almost every page the reflections of the contemporary single-tax discussion" in
  *Capital and Its Earnings*.
- **A wave of academic response.** [Mason Gaffney](/wiki/mason-gaffney/)'s count of at least
  24 works in 28 years directed against George by academic economists.
- **An ironic borrowing.** The paper argues (citing Stabile 1995) that Clark's own marginal
  productivity theory was itself partly derived from George's wage theory — Clark borrowed
  analytical tools from George to build the framework that would erase George's central
  argument.
- **A metaphysical rather than empirical justification.** Quoting Gaffney & Harrison (1994),
  the authors argue Clark's two-factor model rests on endowing "capital with a Platonic
  essence, a deathless soul transcending and surviving its material carcass" — a definitional
  move, not an empirical one.
- **Rhetorical mischaracterization.** Clark's own foreword to *Capital and Its Earnings*
  claims his principles "settle questions of agricultural socialism" — the authors argue
  Clark, who understood George's actual (explicitly anti-nationalization) program, was
  deliberately mischaracterizing it.
- **A telling counter-example.** Alfred Marshall, though critical of George's distribution
  theory, nonetheless favored land value taxation and did not adopt Clark/Fetter/Knight's
  two-factor model — evidence, the authors argue, that the shift to erasing land was a
  specific, contestable choice rather than an inevitable consensus.

## How the Erasure Persisted

The paper argues the erasure was sustained less by ongoing scientific debate than by (a)
academic consensus-building around Clark, Frank Fetter, and Frank Knight; (b) the rise of
socialism as the dominant progressive alternative after World War I, which absorbed George's
audience into a Marxist class framework that does not distinguish land from capital; and (c)
a political realignment around the size of government that displaced the classical
land/labor/capital framing entirely. The authors cite the Mirrlees Review's 2011 puzzlement
at why land value taxation, despite an "almost undeniable" economic case, remains politically
marginal, reading this as further evidence the exclusion is sociological rather than
scientific. The paper's most striking piece of original evidence is a table listing 28
prominent economists — including Nobel laureates Robert Solow, Franco Modigliani, James
Tobin, and William Vickrey — who signed Tideman's own 1990 open letter to Mikhail Gorbachev
advocating land rent socialization, offered as evidence that many mainstream economists
privately accept core elements of George's thesis while avoiding public advocacy.

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This substantially deepens the wiki's existing [land-is-just-capital](/wiki/land-is-just-capital/)
and [marginal productivity](/wiki/marginal-productivity/) coverage, which had summarized
Clark's move briefly; here it becomes a fully argued historical case, co-authored by one of
the wiki's most-cited living Georgist economists. It reframes the standard "land isn't really
distinct from capital" objection not as a settled scientific finding but as a specific,
contested 1880s-90s theoretical choice with an identifiable polemical motive.

## Nuances and Limits

- **A broad historical-philosophical essay, not narrow archival history.** The paper draws
  substantially on secondary sources (Gaffney, Fetter, Stabile, Hudson) rather than
  presenting extensive new primary-source correspondence; its strongest original
  contribution is the Gorbachev-letter signatory table, not new archival material on Clark
  himself.
- **A preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.**
- **Full text read directly (A-claim).** The complete 33-page PDF was obtained via the
  paper's OSF/SocArXiv mirror (HAL's own repository page returned an anti-bot wall to this
  session) and read in full.
