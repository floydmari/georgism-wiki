## Summary

"Land Development Regulation and Housing Affordability: How Rent-Seeking Reallocates
Resources, and the Challenges to Deregulation," by **Ingemar Bengtsson** (Lund University),
is a preliminary draft (dated February 2026) prepared for the Public Choice Society
Conference, San Antonio, March 2026. It applies a formal [rent-seeking](/wiki/rent-seeking/)
incidence framework to land-use regulation — a mechanism the wiki's existing
[NIMBYism](/wiki/nimbyism/) and [housing-supply-restriction](/wiki/planning-restrictions-cause-high-prices/)
coverage describes qualitatively but had not yet decomposed formally in Tullock's terms.

## The Decomposition

Per the paper's abstract, it "examines how land-use regulation facilitates rent-seeking that
harms housing affordability. Using a simple supply-and-demand framework, it decomposes the
effects of stricter regulation on consumer surplus, producer surplus and production costs, in
a Tullock-inspired analysis." The resulting picture of who gains and who loses: **losers**
are households needing housing, some landowners (those barred from developing), blue-collar
construction workers, and subsets of developers; **winners** are existing homeowners,
white-collar professionals (the "compliance industry" of planners, lawyers, and consultants
regulation creates work for), environmental and heritage-interest NGOs, and regulatory
bureaucracies themselves — the latter modeled via a typology (drawing on Mueller 2003) of
budget-maximizing, slack-maximizing, risk-avoiding, and "inside activist" bureaucrats.

## Why Restrictive Regulation Persists

The paper explains persistence through two complementary lenses: **Olson (1982)**'s notion of
institutional sclerosis (concentrated beneficiary groups organize more effectively than
diffuse losers) and **Hayek (1945)**'s spontaneous-order/dispersed-knowledge critique of
centralized land-use planning. It frames the winners' gains through **Tullock's (1967,
2005)** classic rent-seeking "rectangle" — the deadweight loss available for capture by
whichever coalition controls the regulatory process. Reform proposals include: **compensating
landowners** when regulation effectively removes development rights (an explicitly Coasean,
internalization-of-externalities argument, citing Pennington 2000/2002), and **reducing the
number of sequential veto points** — neighbors, councils, agencies, courts, and NGOs — that
each independently shrink the "rectangle" available to a project going forward, since each
additional veto point is itself a place where rent can be extracted or the project killed.

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This gives the wiki's [zoning/rent-seeking cluster](/wiki/rent-seeking/) a rigorous, formally
decomposed account of exactly *who* captures the surplus land-use regulation redistributes —
extending [rent-seeking-drags-growth](/wiki/rent-seeking-drags-growth/)'s general
talent-misallocation argument to the specific institutional structure (veto points, interest-group
coalitions) that makes housing-supply restriction durable even when it's collectively
inefficient. It complements the wiki's [LVT-needs-zoning-reform-first](/wiki/lvt-needs-zoning-reform-first/)
objection page by supplying a public-choice account of *why* zoning reform is politically hard
even when the efficiency case for it is strong.

## Nuances and Limits

- **A preliminary conference draft, not yet peer-reviewed.** Watermarked "Preliminary draft
  March 2026" for the Public Choice Society Conference.
- **A theoretical/analytical decomposition, not new empirical data.** The paper's contribution
  is the formal incidence framework and institutional explanation, built on cited empirical
  literature (Glaeser & Gyourko, Hsieh & Moretti, the Wharton Land Use Regulatory Index)
  rather than original data collection.
- **Full text read directly, independently confirmed by two separate readings (A-claim).**
  The complete 21-page paper was read in full, with the abstract verified verbatim.
