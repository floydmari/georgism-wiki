## Summary

Pawarid Piyajitmetta and Nattapong Puttanapong of Thammasat University, in "Structural path analysis of the Thai economy focusing on land rent" (*Journal of Economic Structures* 14, published 8 December 2025), ask who receives land rent in Thailand and how sectoral growth reaches households through land, labour and capital. They report that land rent income is the most unequally distributed of the three factor incomes and is heavily concentrated among the richest household group. They then test two counterfactuals that shift part of that group's land rent to poorer households.

## Data and Method

- **Accounts.** The authors build a new Social Accounting Matrix from the 2015 input-output table of Thailand's National Economic and Social Development Council (180 sectors aggregated to 18; 51 accounts), National Income accounts and the National Statistical Office household survey. Households are split into five income groups (quintiles).
- **Land separated from capital.** Land rent is estimated from rent per unit area times land area, or as a share of land value. Capital return is operating surplus plus depreciation minus land rent. The government's land income is taken from the "general government's property income receivable" line.
- **Structural path analysis.** Following Defourny and Thorbecke (1984), the method traces how growth in a sector transmits to household groups along paths through land, labour and capital. The five sectors with the highest land rent are agriculture, real estate, manufacturing, accommodation and food services, and mining.
- **Multiplier scenarios.** A multiplier model compares a baseline with two scenarios in which 10% and 25% of the richest group's land rent is reallocated to groups 1 to 4 in shares of 35%, 35%, 25% and 5%.

## Findings

- **Concentration.** Land rent is the most unequally distributed factor income. From agriculture and real estate it reaches only groups 3 to 5; from manufacturing, group 5 alone; from accommodation and food, groups 4 and 5; from mining, groups 3 to 5, mainly group 5.
- **Few channels.** Land has the fewest indirect pathways of the three factors, while capital has the most.
- **Baseline multipliers.** The richest group receives roughly 37 to 40 times as much as the poorest from extra demand in the five sectors.
- **10% scenario.** The ratio of richest to poorest multipliers falls to about 35.
- **25% scenario.** The poorest group's real-estate multiplier rises about 30% (0.0108 to 0.0140) and its agriculture multiplier about 16%. The richest group's multipliers fall by no more than approximately 1%. The richest-to-poorest ratio falls 24% in real estate and 15% in agriculture.

## Relation to the Georgist Case

[Economic rent](/wiki/economic-rent/) is the surplus a site earns beyond what production requires. Once the national accounts separate it from capital returns, the Thai data show it accruing mostly at the top, with few indirect routes to poorer households. On the accounting view that this implies, a levy on land rent would fall chiefly on the richest groups, and the structure fits a long-standing Georgist reading of [land monopoly](/wiki/land-monopoly/) and of land as a [distinctive factor](/wiki/gaffney-land-distinctive-factor/). It parallels [Stiglitz on land, credit and inequality](/wiki/stiglitz-land-credit-inequality/) and [Saez and Zucman on wealth inequality](/wiki/saez-zucman-wealth-inequality/), though those works are not about Thailand.

The introduction cites Laovakul (2016) for a Gini coefficient of 0.88 for land distribution and for the top ten landholders owning 61% of all titled land. These are the paper's citations of that earlier work, not its own estimates.

## Nuances and Limits

- **Not a tax study.** The paper models a reallocation of land rent, not a land value tax, and says nothing about rates, collection, capitalization into land prices or behavioural response.
- **Accounting, not causation.** Structural path analysis and the multiplier model are accounting exercises. The authors state that the paths represent accounting relationships, not causal identification.
- **Modest effects.** The authors describe the reductions in inequality as significant, but the figures are small: a ratio moving from roughly 37 to 40 down to about 35 at 10%, and the richest group's multipliers changing by about 1% at 25%.
- **No price adjustment.** The authors note that the models do not account for price adjustments, that the 2015 table introduces a time lag, and that computable general equilibrium models should be used alongside.
