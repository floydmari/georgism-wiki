## Summary

"Property Rights and Natural Resources," by **Paul Forrester**, appeared in *Law and
Philosophy*, published 20 May 2026. The paper opens with a direct statement of the stakes:
natural resources, especially land, are central to major problems society faces — the
climate crisis, housing shortages, severe inequality — yet land "has been either entirely
neglected or seriously misunderstood by contemporary theorists of distributive justice." The
paper is explicitly Georgist in its argument, citing [Henry George](/wiki/henry-george/)'s
*Progress and Poverty* directly and developing a distinctive original-acquisition theory
built on it.

## The Core Argument: Locke's Conflation

Forrester's starting move is a precise diagnosis of an error in [John
Locke](/wiki/john-locke/)'s labor-mixing theory of property: Locke did not carefully
distinguish between **the value of a natural resource itself** and **the value added by
labor performed upon it**. This conflation is what let Locke conclude that mixing one's
labor with a resource entitles the laborer to both the improvement *and* the resource
itself — a step Forrester argues does not actually follow once the two value components are
properly separated. Following George, Forrester argues instead that **the community**, not
the individual improver, is entitled to the economic value of natural resources, because it
is the community — not any individual's labor — that creates that value (through
population, infrastructure, and economic activity establishing the resource's market worth).

## The Novel Contribution: Turning Nozick Against Himself

The paper's most original move engages Robert Nozick's libertarian objection that taxation
is a form of forced labor — the claim that compelling someone to pay tax on income they
earned through their own effort is morally equivalent to compelling their labor directly.
Forrester argues, following an argument he traces to George's *Progress and Poverty*, that
**self-ownership is not the ground for private appropriation of natural resources, as Locke
assumed — it is actually inconsistent with it.** If genuine self-ownership requires free
access to the natural resources necessary to exercise one's own labor and capacities, then
private ownership of natural resources that excludes others amounts to extracting a
surplus from non-owners who lack that free access — the same coercive structure Nozick
himself condemns as forced labor, now applied in the opposite direction: it is the
*resource owner*, not the tax collector, whose position depends on effectively conscripting
others' labor to pay for access to what should be common. This inverts the standard
libertarian sequence, in which Nozick's forced-labor argument is deployed *against* land
value taxation; Forrester's reading uses the same logical structure to argue *for* it.

## Distinguishing George's View from Left-Libertarianism

Forrester is careful to distinguish the resulting position from **left-libertarianism**, a
related but distinct tradition that also argues for some form of collective claim on natural
resources. The Georgist view Forrester develops holds that **improvers own their
improvements outright** (unlike some left-libertarian positions that qualify improvement
rights too), while **no one is individually entitled to the resource's underlying natural
value** — a sharper, more targeted claim than left-libertarianism's broader redistributive
premises, and one that maps precisely onto the classical Georgist land-value-tax design
(tax the site value, leave improvements untouched).

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This is a rigorous, contemporary academic philosophy paper making the classical Georgist
case in the vocabulary and against the interlocutors (Locke, Nozick) that dominate modern
analytic political philosophy — filling a gap the wiki's existing [John
Locke](/wiki/john-locke/) page identifies but does not fully develop: the page notes Locke's
proviso "concedes the case" for LVT, but had no direct engagement with Nozick's forced-labor
objection, arguably the single most influential modern libertarian argument against
taxation generally. Forrester's paper supplies exactly that missing engagement, and does so
by turning the objection's own logical structure against unconditional natural-resource
ownership rather than by simply asserting a competing value judgment.
