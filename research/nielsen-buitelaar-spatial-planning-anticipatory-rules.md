## Summary

"Spatial planning as eliminating land rents: From symptom treatment to prevention," by
**Victor Mayland Nielsen, Edwin Buitelaar, and Martijn van den Hurk** (Utrecht University),
is an 85-page preprint (submitted to *Progress in Planning*, February 2026, not yet
peer-reviewed). It is one of the most extensively and directly Georgist papers this wiki has
processed: built around 21 numbered "principles" derived substantially from [Henry
George](/wiki/henry-george/)'s *Progress and Poverty* and Ricardo, it reframes [land value
tax](/wiki/land-value-tax/) not as a fiscal or value-capture tool but as a distinct category
of planning instrument.

## Three Kinds of Planning Rule

Building on philosopher Stefano Moroni's (2015) typology, the paper distinguishes three ways
planning rules can be designed:

- **Patterning rules** prescribe specific desired spatial outcomes directly — Dutch
  plot-specific land-use plans, height restrictions, urban growth boundaries. The paper
  critiques these on three grounds: they require predictive knowledge planners cannot
  possess in complex systems ("structural ignorance"); they distort the adaptive,
  discovery-driven efficiency of markets by overprescribing; and they violate rule-of-law
  norms of predictability and impartiality by imposing a single value judgment on everyone.
- **Reactive rules** — the authors' own term — respond quickly to a visible problem without
  diagnosing its root cause: rent control, inclusionary zoning, Amsterdam's office-conversion
  policy responding to visible vacancy. These share the patterning rules' failure modes,
  just via "overresponding" instead of overprescribing.
- **Anticipatory framework rules** — the paper's own theoretical contribution, extending
  Moroni's "framework rules" — are general, simple, preventive rules that filter out harmful
  outcomes before they occur, using only principle-level (Hayekian) knowledge rather than
  specific predictions, without dictating any particular spatial outcome. A minimum-distance
  rule separating industrial from residential land use is the paper's example of the type: it
  prevents a class of harm without specifying where any given use should go.

## LVT as the Paradigm Anticipatory Rule

The paper's central thesis is that land value tax is **the** anticipatory framework rule for
land rent specifically: it "filters out land rent before it manifests itself in social and
economic symptoms," making speculative rent-seeking on land unprofitable at the source rather
than treating downstream symptoms — housing unaffordability, sprawl, financial instability,
inequality — one at a time with reactive tools. The paper organizes its 21 principles into two
blocks: nine on why land rent is a root cause (fixed supply, locational immobility, rent as
unearned by the owner, rent's tendency to absorb the value of productivity gains, and
derivative harms — inequality, speculation-driven financial instability, crowding out
productive investment, sprawl, unaffordability) and twelve on LVT as remedy (capitalization,
[ATCOR and EBCOR](/wiki/atcor/), neutrality, revenue sufficiency, the [Henry George
Theorem](/wiki/henry-george-theorem/), and preventive mechanisms against each symptom
category).

## Case Evidence Marshaled

The paper draws on a wide evidence base already familiar to this wiki: [Pennsylvania
split-rate taxation](/wiki/split-rate-taxation/) and the Pittsburgh building boom
(Oates & Schwab); [Denmark](/wiki/denmark/)'s municipal-border natural experiment showing
full capitalization of land tax into prices; Estonia's land tax dampening housing-bubble
dynamics; and, on the reactive-rule failure side, Beijing height limits cutting land prices
60% and housing output 70% (Ding 2013) and England's green belts raising house prices ~4%
market-wide at a welfare cost of 0.5% of GDP (Koster & Zabihidan 2018).

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This is a genuinely novel theoretical contribution for the wiki: rather than arguing LVT is
efficient or equitable (the wiki's usual framings), it argues LVT belongs to a specific,
philosophically grounded *category* of good institutional design — Hayekian rule-of-law-compatible,
knowledge-economizing prevention — that conventional planning tools (zoning,
rent control, growth boundaries) structurally cannot belong to, because they require either
too much predictive knowledge or too much reactive discretion. It gives the wiki's
[henry-george-theorem](/wiki/henry-george-theorem/) and [land value
tax](/wiki/land-value-tax/) pages a planning-theory register they previously lacked.

## Nuances and Limits

- **A preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.** Submitted to *Progress in Planning*, February 2026.
- **Honest about open empirical questions.** The paper itself flags that ATCOR/EBCOR remain
  "largely empirically unexplored," that LVT's distributional (progressive vs. regressive)
  findings are mixed and methodologically contested, and that LVT's theorized effect on
  "sound" vs. "unsound" speculation (via Dwyer 2014, Tideman) is not fully tested at scale.
  This page preserves that nuance rather than presenting the paper as an uncritical
  endorsement.
- **Full text read directly (A-claim).** The complete 85-page preprint was obtained (via an
  alternate Utrecht repository URL after the standard download link was blocked) and read in
  full.

## Bears On

- **Concept:** [Henry George Theorem](/wiki/henry-george-theorem/) — reframes the theorem's logic in planning-theory terms as a category of good institutional design, not just a fiscal result.
- **Concept:** [Land Value Tax](/wiki/land-value-tax/) — the paradigm case of an "anticipatory framework rule," argued at length from Hayekian and Georgist first principles together.
- **Objection:** [LVT Needs Zoning Reform First](/wiki/lvt-needs-zoning-reform-first/) — offers a theoretical account of why patterning-rule-style zoning is itself a structurally flawed planning approach LVT-style anticipatory rules should replace, not merely precede.
