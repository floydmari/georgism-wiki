## Summary

"Landlord opposition to rent control and the politics of class monopoly rent," by **Matthew
B. Anderson, Graham Zickefoose, Tate Andrie, and Tony Newton**, appeared in *Human
Geography* 18(1): 46–58 (2024). It is the third entry the wiki now covers in Anderson's
"class monopoly rent" series — after [Kyoto's cultural monopoly
rent](/wiki/anderson-kyoto-cultural-monopoly-rent/) and [Copenhagen's state monopoly
rent](/wiki/anderson-dascher-copenhagen-land-rent/) — but with a distinct empirical focus:
US landlord political mobilization rather than an urban-development case study.

## The Argument

The paper analyzes landlord associations' opposition to **Oregon's 2019 statewide rent
control legislation (SB 608)**, arguing that their opposition strategy "represents deployment
of 'class monopoly rent' concepts as political leverage." Per the abstract, landlord
associations collectively advanced arguments that "effectively constitutes the discursive
deployment of class monopoly power as a political strategy to lobby against tenant rights
proponents." This inverts the concept's usual direction: **class monopoly rent** — the idea
that landlords, as a class controlling a fixed housing stock, can extract rent above what
individual competitive market dynamics alone would predict — is typically deployed by critics
*against* landlords. Here, landlord associations turn the same conceptual toolkit around,
arguing (in economic-sounding terms) that rent control itself distorts markets and harms
housing supply, effectively using rent theory's own vocabulary as a rhetorical shield.

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This is a genuinely distinct contribution from the wiki's other two Anderson pages: where
Kyoto examines selective commodification of heritage character and Copenhagen examines a
state development corporation's own rent-extraction incentives, this paper is about the
**political rhetoric of landlord opposition** to a specific, real US policy fight — directly
relevant to the wiki's [rent-seeking](/wiki/rent-seeking/) and [land
monopoly](/wiki/land-monopoly/) coverage of how organized capital defends extractive
positions politically, not just economically.

## Nuances and Limits

- **A single US state case study (Oregon, 2019).** Findings about landlord-association
  rhetoric in this specific legislative fight may not generalize to rent-control debates
  elsewhere.
- **Draws on Marxian rent theory and housing-market sociology**, a related but distinct
  tradition from the wiki's core Georgist economic-rent framing — the paper's "class monopoly
  rent" concept, like the wiki's other Anderson-series pages, uses "rent" in this broader
  critical-geography sense.
- **Abstract-level source (B-claim).** The publisher page yielded only site-template
  content; this page is built from the verbatim abstract obtained via the Crossref API. The
  scanner's original metadata for this item omitted the paper's fourth co-author (Tony
  Newton) and misstated the publication year as 2025; corrected here to the confirmed 2024
  publication.
