## Summary

"Does Progressivity Raise Tax Capacity? Experimental Evidence from the D.R. Congo," by **Gabriel Tourek**, **Arthur Laroche**, **Augustin Bergeron**, **Joana Naritomi**, **Jonathan Weigel**, and **Marina Mavungu Ngoma**, circulated as [NBER Working Paper 35536](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35536) in July 2026 (DOI 10.3386/w35536). It comes from the same research team behind the wiki's existing [DRC state-capacity-ceiling study](/wiki/bergeron-tourek-weigel-drc-property-tax/), and extends that agenda from the *level* of property-tax rates to their *structure*: in partnership with a provincial government, neighborhoods of a major Congolese city were randomly assigned to either a **progressive** or a **proportional** property-tax schedule.

## The Experiment and Findings

- **Progressivity raised revenue substantially.** The progressive schedule increased revenue by **56% relative to the proportional one**.
- **The gains came from both ends of the schedule.** At the top, higher statutory rates raised revenue despite some compliance losses; at the bottom, lower rates prompted compliance gains large enough to offset the reduced liabilities.
- **Taxpayers responded to their own rates.** Individuals reacted primarily to their personal tax rate, not to others' rates or to perceptions of the overall schedule's fairness.
- **But effective rates were regressive — most so under the progressive schedule.** Taxes actually paid as a share of property value *declined* with property value, and the abstract states this pattern was most pronounced under the progressive schedule. Statutory progressivity did not deliver effective progressivity on its own, because compliance among high-value owners fell short of their higher scheduled rates.
- **Targeted enforcement, not the schedule, reversed that.** Only after the progressive schedule was scaled citywide in subsequent years, with enforcement targeted at high-value properties, did the pattern reverse and statutory and effective rates come into alignment — shifting the burden toward wealthier owners.

The authors' conclusion is that progressive property taxation can raise fiscal capacity in low-income settings when paired with strategic enforcement — it is not merely an equity choice purchased at a revenue cost.

## Relation to the Georgist Case

The [companion Econometrica study](/wiki/bergeron-tourek-weigel-drc-property-tax/) established that weak enforcement capacity puts a ceiling on the revenue-maximizing property-tax *rate*; this paper shows the *shape* of the schedule is a further, underused lever — and that the compliance responses which create the ceiling behave asymmetrically across the property-value distribution. For the Georgist program the notable result is that **recurrent taxation of property can be made progressive without sacrificing revenue — indeed while raising it** — in exactly the low-capacity settings where property registries and valuations are weakest. That bears on the standing debate over whether land-and-property taxation is regressive or progressive in incidence (see [Land value tax can be progressive](/wiki/land-value-tax-can-be-progressive/)): here progressivity was not an incidence side-effect but a design choice that itself expanded fiscal capacity.

The result cuts against the Georgist case in one respect that should not be glossed. A progressive *statutory* schedule produced *regressive* effective rates — by the abstract's own account, the most regressive of the schedules tested. Anyone citing this paper for the proposition that recurrent property taxation can be made progressive has to carry the enforcement condition with it: progressivity on paper was not progressivity in incidence until the state separately invested in collecting from high-value owners. That is a caution about administrative capacity, and it applies with equal force to a land-only tax, whose valuations in such settings would be no easier to enforce.

The usual caveats also apply: this is a working paper, not yet peer-reviewed; the tax studied is a general property tax rather than a land-only tax; and the revenue result is one city in one campaign.

## Bears On

- **Benefit (supports, with caveat):** [Higher property-tax rates raise welfare in developing countries](/wiki/property-tax-raises-welfare-developing/) — experimental evidence that schedule design, not just rate level, expands fiscal capacity in low-income states.
- **Benefit (mixed — supports and qualifies):** [Land value tax can be progressive](/wiki/land-value-tax-can-be-progressive/) — progressive recurrent property taxation proved administrable and revenue-positive in a very low-capacity setting, but effective rates were regressive under the progressive schedule until targeted enforcement was added. Cite for the design-feasibility point, not for progressive incidence on its own.
- **Research:** [Bergeron, Tourek & Weigel (2024), the state-capacity ceiling](/wiki/bergeron-tourek-weigel-drc-property-tax/) — the companion study on rate levels and enforcement from the same team and setting.
