## Summary

"Private Government at Home: Landlord Power and Rental Residential Domination in the United
States," by **Shai Karp** (Northwestern University), appeared in *Politics & Society*
(2024). It brings a political-theory register the wiki has not previously applied directly to
[landlord-tenant](/wiki/landlords-cannot-pass-lvt-to-tenants/) relations: rather than treating
housing insecurity as a market-failure or affordability problem, it argues rental housing is
a site of **domination** — unaccountable power that structurally limits tenant autonomy —
drawing on philosopher Elizabeth Anderson's concept of **private government**, extended
through radical republican and feminist theory.

## Structural and Interpersonal Domination

Per the paper's own abstract: "rental housing is a sphere where people are subject to
substantial unaccountable power in ways that drastically limit their autonomy." The paper
develops this at two levels. **Structural power** stems from the US state's near-total
delegation of housing provision to private markets, giving landlords outsized cultural and
political influence — including the ability to credibly threaten that tenant protections will
shrink housing supply. **Interpersonal power** extends beyond the familiar threat of eviction
into more paternalistic territory: landlords' access to tenants' intimate domestic spaces and
personal data enables surveillance and harassment, while chronically unenforced maintenance
obligations manufacture tenant dependence on delinquent owners who face little consequence
for neglect.

## Countervailing Power, Not Just Rent Control

The paper's proposed remedy is distinctive: rather than centering rent control, Karp argues
for **"countervailing power"** built through legally protected tenant organizing — "an
institutional framework legitimizing tenant unions, protecting organizing activity, and
ensuring a right to bargain and strike," explicitly modeled on the **National Labor Relations
Act**, adapted from labor to housing. The specific political-theory move is reframing the
core harm as the *power relation itself* — arbitrary, unaccountable landlord authority — rather
than any particular unfair price, which is why the remedy targets the power asymmetry
directly (via organizing rights) rather than capping its output (via price controls).

## Relation to the Georgist Case

This is a distinct register from the wiki's existing landlord/tenant material: [landlords
cannot pass LVT to tenants](/wiki/landlords-cannot-pass-lvt-to-tenants/) addresses tax
incidence economics, and [rental financialization](/wiki/august-rental-financialization/)
and [financialization of land](/wiki/financialization-of-land/) address ownership
concentration and REIT structures — none engage the political-theory question of domination
directly. It is also a useful contrast to [Anderson, Zickefoose, Andrie & Newton's
class-monopoly-rent paper](/wiki/anderson-landlord-rent-control-class-monopoly/), which
examines landlord political *rhetoric* against rent control; Karp's paper instead builds
the underlying normative case for why landlord power over tenants is itself the problem,
independent of any specific policy fight.

## Nuances and Limits

- **A political-theory contribution, not an empirical study.** The paper's argument is
  normative and conceptual; it does not present new quantitative data on landlord practices.
- **US-focused**, reflecting the specific institutional and legal context (the near-total
  private delegation of housing provision) the argument is built on.
- **Abstract-level for the peer-reviewed article itself (A-claim for the verbatim abstract;
  B-claim for supporting mechanism detail).** The publisher page returned only template
  content; the verbatim published abstract was obtained via the Crossref API (matching an
  independently cached Semantic Scholar copy word-for-word), and additional structural/

  author's own companion public writing on the same argument (a LPE Project blog post), not
  the peer-reviewed article's own body text, which remains paywalled with no open-access copy
  found.
