## Overview

California's Proposition 13, approved by voter initiative in June 1978, added **Article XIII A** to the California Constitution: it capped the ad valorem property tax at one percent of "full cash value" and limited annual assessment increases until a property changes hands.[1] It is the canonical property-tax revolt event in the United States, and is treated in the comparative land-value taxation literature as a major political counterpoint to LVT advocacy, illustrating how incumbent property owners can resist or roll back land-based taxation.[5]

## Mechanics

Proposition 13 instituted two core restrictions on California property taxation:

1. **Rate cap.** Article XIII A, §1 limits the tax on real property to "One percent (1%) of the full cash value of such property."[1] This was a sharp reduction from the pre-existing regime, under which California property was taxed at locally set rates averaging roughly 2.5–3% of assessed value.[2]

2. **Acquisition-value assessment.** "Full cash value" is defined (§2) as the 1975–76 assessed value or, thereafter, "the appraised value of real property when purchased, newly constructed, or a change in ownership has occurred"; that base value may then rise to reflect inflation "not to exceed 2 percent for any given year."[1] A property is thus reassessed to market value only on sale, new construction, or change of ownership.

This system is known as **acquisition-value assessment** (or "welcome stranger" assessment): the county assessor sets a property's taxable value at its purchase price, so long-tenured owners pay on values far below market while new purchasers pay on full market value — creating large disparities between identically situated properties.[2] The U.S. Supreme Court upheld these disparities against an equal-protection challenge in *Nordlinger v. Hahn* (1992).[3]

## Lock-In Effects

Because moving triggers reassessment at market value, Proposition 13 creates a powerful **lock-in effect**: long-tenured homeowners face a large implicit tax penalty for selling and buying an equivalent home, discouraging mobility. Two empirical studies quantify it. Wasi & White (2005) find that from 1970 to 2000 the average tenure length of California owners rose by about 1.04 years relative to comparison states, an effect attributable to Prop 13.[4] NBER's own digest of that paper adds three breakdowns worth carrying, because they complicate the intuitive reading of who bears the lock-in. **Renters' tenure rose more in proportional terms than owners'** — 0.79 years, or 19%, against owners' 10% — even though renters hold no Prop 13 tax base themselves, which the authors attribute to the tax advantage's indirect effect on the rental market. The effect was **larger for African-American households** (1.3 years for owners and 1.8 for renters, 1970–90) and **larger for out-of-state migrants** than for native-born residents (1.5 years owners / 0.6 renters, 1970–2000). And it varied sharply by geography, from **under 1 year of added tenure in inland California cities to over 2 years in Los Angeles and roughly 3 years in the Bay Area** — that is, the lock-in bites hardest exactly where land values rose fastest and the gap between assessed and market value grew widest.[14] Ferreira (2010), exploiting Propositions 60/90 (which let over-55 owners carry their tax base to a new home), finds that mobility among 55-year-old owners is roughly 25% higher than among otherwise-similar 54-year-olds — direct evidence that the lock-in is caused by the loss of the tax benefit on moving.[6]

A 2026 NBER working paper extends the lock-in finding from *mobility* to *who lives in the housing stock*. [Coven, Golder, Gupta & Ndiaye](/wiki/coven-property-taxes-housing-allocation/) build an overlapping-generations model in which raising California's property taxes to Texas levels increases young homeownership while decreasing elderly homeownership, through two channels: capitalization into lower prices cuts the downpayment a financially constrained buyer must raise, while higher annual obligations raise holding costs for older owners. They also find that removing the step-up basis at death lowers elderly homeownership, which they read as evidence that incumbent tenure is partly sustained by bequest tax advantages. The relevance to Prop 13 is direct — the paper's California calibration is a calibration of *this* regime — though the result is a model-based simulation rather than a quasi-experimental estimate like Wasi & White's or Ferreira's, and the paper is not yet peer-reviewed.[11]

The lock-in effect is conceptually related to the [transitional gains trap](/wiki/transitional-gains-trap/): the tax savings from Proposition 13 capitalized into incumbent property values, so that any future repeal would impose losses on current owners — making the policy politically difficult to reverse even decades later. Christopher England applies the same Tullockian transitional-gains-trap logic to a parallel property-tax revolt — the end of Vancouver's land-value-only rating — arguing that rating benefits and burdens became embedded in property values in ways that shaped political resistance.[7]

## LVT-Politics Lessons

For Georgist advocacy, Proposition 13 illustrates several political dynamics:

- **Incumbent resistance to land-based taxation.** The revolt demonstrated that existing property owners can mobilize overwhelming political force to cap or reduce taxes on land and improvements, even when the pre-existing system was closer to a market-value property tax. England argues that a structurally similar dynamic — incumbents defending capitalized rating advantages — ended Vancouver's land-value-only rating.[7]

- **Tax capitalization cuts both ways.** As documented on this wiki's [tax capitalization](/wiki/tax-capitalization/) page, cutting taxes on land raises its price. Proposition 13's tax cut thus capitalized into higher California property prices — benefiting owners at the time of passage but raising the cost of entry for subsequent buyers. This is the mirror image of the [LVT transition wealth shock](/wiki/lvt-transition-wealth-shock/): a tax cut produces a one-time windfall for incumbents and higher prices for newcomers, just as a tax increase produces a one-time loss for incumbents and lower prices for newcomers.

- **Assessment limits distort the base.** By freezing assessments below market value, Proposition 13 undermined the informational function that accurate assessment plays in any property-tax system — including a potential [land value tax](/wiki/land-value-tax/). The resulting disparities illustrate why assessment quality is treated as the binding practical constraint in LVT implementation, as discussed on the [land cannot be assessed](/wiki/land-cannot-be-assessed/) objection page. The definitive public-finance analysis of the acquisition-value system is O'Sullivan, Sexton & Sheffrin's *Property Taxes and Tax Revolts: The Legacy of Proposition 13* (1995) and their follow-up study: because a household's tax liability tracks its purchase price rather than current market value, "a homeowner in a recently purchased dwelling will pay more taxes than a homeowner who purchased an identical dwelling some time earlier" — a large, persistent horizontal inequity that grows with house-price appreciation.[9]

- **The revolt as a cautionary tale for transition design.** The political backlash that produced Proposition 13 followed a period of rapidly rising property assessments driven by inflation and housing-price appreciation. This underscores the importance of phased transition and relief mechanisms in any move toward heavier land taxation — the same concern addressed in the [LVT transition wealth shock](/wiki/lvt-transition-wealth-shock/) objection.

## A 2025 Reform Proposal

A student policy brief in the Cornell Policy Group's *Journal of Public Affairs* (Fall 2025) proposes replacing Proposition 13 with a revenue-neutral land value tax rather than repealing it outright.[10] The brief's core empirical claim, drawn from a Syracuse University Maxwell School model, is that an LVT "could increase housing development by between 14% and 32%" in California.[10] It also reports a live legislative angle: Assemblymember Alex Lee had previously authored Assembly Bill 362 to study a statewide land value tax (the bill died in committee in January 2024), which the brief reads as signaling Lee "would be a credible sponsor for a constitutional amendment" reforming Prop 13.[10] The brief proposes a phased, two-tiered system modeled on Harrisburg and Allentown, Pennsylvania, designed to be revenue-neutral so most existing homeowners would not face higher bills.[10] (This is a policy-advocacy brief rather than peer-reviewed research; the Maxwell School estimate and the AB 362 status are reported as the brief states them and have not been independently checked against the primary sources.)

## Legacy and Spread

Proposition 13 helped touch off a wider tax-limitation wave; the best-known parallel is Massachusetts's **Proposition 2½ (1980)**, which caps a locality's total property levy at 2.5% of assessed value and limits annual levy growth to 2.5%. In California itself the measure remains in force but has been amended by later ballot initiatives — notably **Propositions 60 and 90** (base-year transfers for over-55 owners) and **Proposition 19 (2020)**, which broadened those transfers while narrowing the inherited-property exclusion.

## The 2026 Revolt Wave

A Lincoln Institute explainer published in October 2026, ahead of that November's state ballots, treats the current round of property-tax measures as a successor to the 1970s and 1980s wave that Proposition 13 and Massachusetts' Proposition 2½ led. It ties the new revolt to home prices that "have skyrocketed more than 50 percent since January 2020", while noting that property taxes nationwide have risen only slightly faster than inflation over the same period, and describes a locality that leaves its rate unchanged as values rise as producing "a silent tax increase".[15] The truth-in-taxation laws it recommends work by naming that increase: Texas requires each taxing unit to publish a "no-new-revenue" rate, "generally equal to last year's taxes divided by the current taxable value of properties that were also on the tax roll last year", and to hold an election if it adopts more than 3.5 per cent above it, and Georgia defines a "rollback rate" as the previous year's millage adjusted for the value added by reassessment of existing property, so that any rate above it must be advertised as a tax increase.[27] The measures it names are Florida's Amendment 3, which proposed to raise the non-school homestead exemption to $250,000 by 2028, cut the assessment cap on non-homestead property from 10 to 5 per cent and require the legislature to set a uniform procedure by which counties and municipalities may raise the exemption further; a North Carolina constitutional amendment requiring the legislature to enact laws "limiting the amount by which the levy of taxes on property may increase", with no specifics; and [Wyoming](/wiki/wyoming/)'s Initiative 1, which proposed to exempt half of every homestead's assessed value. An Ohio amendment to abolish the tax "fell short of making the ballot".[15] The explainer ranks assessment limits of the Proposition 13 kind as "one of the most problematic" responses, illustrating with two Los Angeles houses of equal value paying $5,212 and $11,172, and prefers circuit breakers, deferrals, moderate homestead exemptions and truth-in-taxation laws. Its one remark on land is the efficiency point that the part of the tax falling on unimproved land does not distort behaviour because "the supply of land can't be increased or reduced in response to a tax"; it does not discuss land value taxation as a response.[15]

Florida's measure is the largest of the three. Placed on the ballot by the legislature as CS/HJR 1F, which passed the Senate 30 to 9 and the House 75 to 26 on 2 June 2026, it exempts the first $150,000 of a homestead's value from non-school taxes in 2027 and $250,000 from 2028, indexed to inflation thereafter, leaving the Save Our Homes cap and the 10-mill limit in place.[16][17] Voters approved it on 3 November 2026 with about 62 per cent, clearing the 60 per cent that Florida requires for constitutional amendments.[26] The state's Revenue Estimating Conference puts the combined local non-school revenue loss from the exemption and the new 5 per cent cap at $4.93 billion in 2027–28, $8.71 billion in 2028–29 and $11.83 billion a year once fully phased in.[16] On 3 August 2026 a Leon County circuit judge found the original ballot title and summary "clearly and conclusively defective", and the Attorney General filed revised language rather than appeal.[18] The Tax Foundation reads the measure as a restructuring of the local tax base away from homeowners, and a consultant's estimate it relies on puts the increase on rental property at about 15 per cent by 2031 if local governments raise millage to recover the lost revenue; both are the analysts' own calculations, and the latter is published with the support of housing and apartment associations.[19]

Two measures on the same ballot illustrate the two directions a legislature can take. **Oklahoma's State Question 847**, referred by the legislature as SJR 39 (Senate 38 to 8 on 26 March 2026, House 80 to 14 on 15 April), proposed to tighten the assessment limit adopted under SQ 758 in 2012, lowering the cap on annual growth in fair cash value from 3 to 1.75 per cent for homesteads and agricultural land and from 5 to 4 per cent for other property from tax year 2027, and to replace the "senior freeze" with an income-tiered limit: no increase for owners aged 65 or over with household income at or below the county or metropolitan median, 0.35, 0.7, 1.05 and 1.4 per cent for successive twenty-point bands up to 180 per cent of median, and the standard 1.75 per cent above that. The Attorney General found the legislature's ballot title deficient and issued a rewritten title on 3 June 2026.[20] Voters rejected the question on 3 November 2026, 54 to 46 per cent.[26] **Iowa**, by contrast, enacted Senate File 2472, signed on 18 May 2026, which from the fiscal year beginning 1 July 2027 limits each rate-limited local levy to a rate that raises 102 per cent of the prior year's certified dollars on the existing base, with value attributable to new valuation excluded from the divisor for the main county and city levies; the former fixed rate caps resume as a ceiling from 1 July 2030, and voter-approved rate increases are honoured.[21] The Tax Foundation's survey of the November ballots reads the assessment-limit measures as shifting burdens onto non-favoured property and the levy-limit model as the "neutral" alternative; that is the think-tank's own judgement, but it matches the mechanism described above, under which a limit on collections rather than on assessed values leaves the base intact and the relative tax on land and buildings undistorted.[22]

The 3 November 2026 results were mixed. Besides Florida's Amendment 3, voters approved North Carolina's levy-limit amendment with about 60 per cent, Tennessee's Amendment 2 barring a state property tax, which the state does not levy, with about 67 per cent, Georgia's Amendment 1 widening the acreage limit for conservation-use farm and timber land, and three of Louisiana's five property-tax amendments, on a surviving spouse's veteran exemption, a senior exemption and a higher income limit for the special assessment level. They rejected Oklahoma's State Question 847, Louisiana's Amendment 2 on rate limits and Amendment 10 on rehabilitated property, Florida's Amendment 2 exempting agricultural tangible property, and Wyoming's Initiative 1, which lost 56 to 44 per cent in the first citizen initiative the state had voted on in three decades. The two defeats that matter most for the pattern above are the two measures that would have cut deepest into the base: Oklahoma's tighter assessment cap and Wyoming's exemption of half of every home's value.[26]

A National Association of Counties report of 30 October 2026, written from the standpoint of the governments that collect the tax, counts the legislation behind the wave. By the association's tally, state legislatures enacted at least 168 property-tax actions in 40 states and the District of Columbia between January 2020 and June 2026, 72 per cent of them relief measures such as exemptions, credits and refunds, while only four states and the District tightened a rate or levy limit and ten changed how property is assessed. It puts the revenue loss from the actions that carry fiscal notes at $15.3 billion in the first fiscal year after passage and $14.5 billion in the second, a figure that excludes 34 ballot measures and six bills with no notes. Its own calculation from Census survey and consumer-price data finds that the median bill rose 57 per cent, or $1,168, between 2010 and 2024 but only 9 per cent, or $189, in 2010 dollars, and that the effective rate on owner-occupied housing fell about 18 per cent, which is the Lincoln explainer's "silent tax increase" seen from the other side of the ledger. The report records that at least fifteen states have discussed abolishing the tax outright since 2024 and that none has enacted it, and its stake is explicit: property taxes are the largest single source of county revenue, while state governments collect about 3 per cent of the national total.[23][25] The abolition campaigns can be checked against the record. North Dakota's Measure 4 was defeated 63 to 37 per cent in November 2024; Ohio's petition committee suspended its 2026 effort on 5 June 2026 and is aiming at the 2027 ballot; Michigan's organisers announced on 22 May 2026 that they had not gathered enough signatures; South Dakota's initiative missed its 3 February filing deadline; and Florida's HJR 203, which passed the House 80 to 30 in February, did not reach the ballot. None of the twelve property-tax measures put to voters on 3 November 2026 abolishes a local property tax.[24]
