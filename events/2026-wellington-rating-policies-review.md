## Overview

From **19 October to 15 November 2026**, Wellington City Council is consulting on five proposed changes to its rating policies.[2] The first asks whether the **general rate** should be set on **land value** instead of **capital value**, the base used "since the late 1990s".[1] Land value excludes "the value of houses, apartments, commercial buildings and other improvements".[1] The Council's planning and finance committee identified consulting on that move as its preferred option in June 2026. Two other proposals bear on the same question: cutting the commercial differential from **3.7:1 to 3.0:1** over six years, and extending the area covered by the **5.0×** vacant-land and derelict-building differential.[1]

**No decision has been made.** The Council "may decide to proceed with all, some, or none of the proposals, or make other changes".[7] Everything below describes a proposal under consultation, as at 23 October 2026.

## How the Proposal Arose

On **25 June 2026**, Te Taurapa | Council Planning and Finance Committee considered officers' report on rating changes for the 2027-37 Long-term Plan. It resolved to "consult on changing the rating base for general rates to Land Value (LV) from Capital Value (CV)", labelled "Option 2 [preferred]", by **16 votes to 2**; the alternative was to discontinue the review.[3][4] On **1 October 2026** it adopted the consultation document by 13 votes to 3, adding the statement that "Retaining the status quo, or other differentials are also viable options".[1][5]

The Council's stated reason is that land value rating "may encourage land to be developed and used more efficiently", since owners "do not pay higher general rates simply because they build new homes".[1] It adds that advocates argue the base "can discourage land banking and under-utilisation", cites a 2015 Productivity Commission view that councils should consider its merits, and says land value is "a better proxy for income than capital value".[1] The document also records the case for capital value: higher-value properties are said to place greater demands on services.[1]

The Council is candid about the limits of its evidence. It "cannot model any increased development, reduced land banking, or increased housing supply" from the switch, "due to a lack of other Councils of similar size undertaking such a transition recently".[6] The efficiency rationale is therefore a stated expectation, not a modelled result.

## What Would Change

- **General rates on land value.** Total revenue would not change; the burden would be redistributed. Sites with high land value and little development "could pay a greater share", intensively developed sites "a smaller share", outdoor car parks would pay more, and utilities "could pay considerably less" because much of their capital value is infrastructure.[1] The sector and Downtown targeted rates would stay on capital value.[1]
- **Who gains and loses.** A presentation tabled on 25 June reported modelling in which about **60% of residential ratepayers** would be better off, mainly in inner-city apartments and the outer suburbs, and about **80% of commercial ratepayers** worse off, "mainly driven by utilities benefitting".[4]
- **The commercial multiple.** Holding the current 51:49 residential–commercial split under land value would need a commercial differential of about **5.8**; the document's examples assume the split continues.[1]
- **Cutting the differential (a separate proposal).** On 2026/27 rates, moving from 3.7:1 to 3.0:1 would reduce the general rate on an average $1.5 million downtown commercial property from **$18,280 to $16,704** and raise that on a $1.5 million residential property from **$4,460 to $4,861**; these figures are on the existing capital-value base. The Council notes it "remains uncertain" whether landlords would pass a reduction on to tenants.[1]
- **Vacant land.** The inner-city differential introduced in the 2024-34 Long-term Plan charges **5.0 times** the base general rate. The proposal extends it to undeveloped property along Adelaide Road, including around the Basin Reserve and up to Wellington Regional Hospital.[1] Officers had described such an extension as a possible "limited alternative to a land value rating system".[3]
- **Cost.** Implementing land value rating is estimated at **$600,000 to $2 million**, met through rates.[1]

The consultation document illustrates the shift with seven hypothetical rating units, holding the residential–commercial split at 51:49 with a 5.8 commercial differential and showing estimated 2026/27 Council rates (GST included, excluding water, regional council and sludge charges) under each base:[1]

| Rating unit | Capital value base | Land value base |
|---|---|---|
| Hillside house, 1 unit | $3,351 | $3,714 |
| Apartment building, 39 units | $37,958 | $35,095 |
| Residential, 1 unit | $4,309 | $3,626 |
| Residential duplex, 2 units | $4,446 | $5,437 |
| Mixed use, 5 units | $16,752 | $16,430 |
| Industrial/commercial, 1 unit | $33,487 | $82,748 |
| CBD mixed use, 27 units | $73,041 | $56,767 |

The direction is as the Council describes: dense buildings on valuable land pay less, and a single-unit industrial site on a large plot pays far more.

## The National Context

The consultation document reports that **65 per cent** of New Zealand councils (51 of 78) rate on capital value in 2026, against 58 per cent ten years earlier and 50 per cent twenty years earlier; Porirua, Hutt City and Upper Hutt use capital value.[1][4] A briefing slide lists Hutt City, Palmerston North and Thames-Coromandel among recent moves towards capital value.[4]

That fits the longer record on the [New Zealand page](/wiki/new-zealand/#decline-to-capital-value-rating). Its figures (29 per cent of councils on land value in 2019) come from a different analysis and are not combined with the Council's series here. The best-known test of the base, Auckland's 2010 amalgamation, found little effect on new building when councils moved the other way; see [Gemmell, Grimes and Skidmore](/wiki/gemmell-grimes-skidmore-nz/).

## Timeline

- **19 October to 15 November 2026:** consultation; oral hearings in November.[1][8]
- **December 2026:** after analysing feedback, the committee votes on whether "all, some, or none of the five proposals" go forward.[8][9]
- **March-April 2027:** formal consultation on the draft 2027-37 Long-term Plan (a Council release says April).[5][9]
- **June 2027:** Long-term Plan adoption.[3]
- **1 July 2027:** the earliest effect, and "only if the Council adopts" the changes after that process.[1] A transition plan, possibly phased, would be considered in the Plan.[1][6]

The Council flags regional amalgamation as a risk: officers advised that "it may not be the right time" to diverge from neighbouring councils that rate on capital value.[3]

## Relation to the Georgist Case

A capital city is consulting on reversing a drift that the [New Zealand](/wiki/new-zealand/) page records over some forty years. The Council's rationale, that a base excluding improvements does not penalise building and may discourage [land banking](/wiki/land-banking/), is the standard efficiency argument for [split-rate taxation](/wiki/split-rate-taxation/) and the [land value tax](/wiki/land-value-tax/). The extended vacant-land differential is a use-based penalty on idle sites, a cruder cousin of the continuous incentive a land base provides; compare [speculative vacancy](/wiki/speculative-vacancy/).

This is a change of **rating base for the general rate**, not a new tax. Total revenue is unchanged, commercial land still pays at a differential, targeted rates stay on capital value, and the same consultation proposes lowering the commercial multiple.

## Nuances and Limits

- **Nothing is decided.** The June "preferred" label applies to consulting on the change, not to adopting it; the Council's FAQ says "No decision has yet been made".[8]
- **The modelling is illustrative.** The 60% and 80% figures come from a tabled presentation, not a published study, and the Council states that key modelling will be subject to peer review.[4][10] The dollar examples above are the Council's own hypothetical rating units, not a sample of actual properties.
- **Legal basis.** The Council states that general rates are set under section 13 of the Local Government (Rating) Act 2002.[11] The Productivity Commission observed in 2019 that councils other than Auckland may choose land value under current law (see [New Zealand](/wiki/new-zealand/)).
- **Advocates.** Common Ground Aotearoa, an advocacy group named in the 25 June minutes as a participant and proponent, is mentioned as the origin of advocacy for the change only, not as evidence for it.[4]
